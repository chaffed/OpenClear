Login, SSO & Account Lockout

OpenClear supports password login (with optional passkey step-up) out of the box, plus single sign-on your organization can configure.

{{ docs.screenshot("login", "Login screen") }}

Single sign-on

Configure an SSO connection at the tenant level from Settings → SSO, or at the customer level from a customer's own detail page, if you want a specific customer's users to authenticate through their own identity provider instead of a password.

A connection uses OpenID Connect (Okta, Entra ID, Ping, Google, Keycloak) or SAML 2.0 (ADFS, Shibboleth, and the same providers). Either way, people get access through the connection's group mappings, re-checked at every sign-in. Each connection's page lists the URLs to give your identity provider. For SAML, that includes its metadata URL.

  • Directory provisioning (SCIM 2.0). Turn it on from the connection's page. It gives you a base URL and a token for your directory. The directory can then create, update and deactivate people, and push its groups. Deactivating someone there ends their OpenClear sessions.
  • Signing out at your identity provider ends the person's OpenClear sessions too. This works through OIDC back-channel logout or SAML single logout.
  • An OpenClear tile in your identity provider's portal can start sign-in. For OIDC, use the connection's "initiate login" URL. For SAML, turn on "Allow sign-in started at the identity provider".
  • Your online banking platform as the identity provider. Set a customer claim on a bank-wide connection. The claim carries each person's customer number, and their session is limited to that customer. The platform's own server can exchange a signed-in person's ID token for a token acting for them. To allow that, link its API client to the connection.
  • Checking the provider used MFA. People who sign in through single sign-on don't need an OpenClear security key: their identity provider is expected to require a second factor. To have OpenClear check that rather than trust it, turn on Require the identity provider to report MFA on the connection. A sign-in is then refused unless the provider says MFA was used (OIDC amr containing mfa, or an acr or SAML authentication context OpenClear recognises, such as aal2 or Entra's multipleauthn). If your provider uses its own values, such as a Keycloak level 2, list them on the connection.

Customers' own sign-on

A customer you let manage its own sign-on (its page, Self-service) can connect its identity provider itself, for its own people, from Settings → Sign-on. Its connections map only to the roles you offer to customers. Because a customer's identity provider can claim any email address, it creates people automatically (on sign-in or through SCIM) only in email domains the customer has verified with a DNS record; anyone else is invited first. The customer can turn off password sign-in only after signing in through its connection, and you can always turn it back on from the customer's sign-on page.

Someone who signs in through a customer's connection stays in that customer: to reach another organization they belong to, they sign out and sign in there. One customer's identity provider never vouches for anywhere else.

API clients

To let one of your systems (your core, your online banking provider, your own middleware) use OpenClear's API, create an API client under Admin → API clients rather than giving it a person's sign-in. Choose a security group for what it may do and, if it should only see one customer's data, that customer. Copy the secret when it's shown: it's shown only once.

  • The system exchanges its client id and secret for a token that lasts about half an hour, at POST /api/v1/oauth/token, and asks for a new one when it expires.
  • Everything it does appears in the audit log under the client's name.
  • Rotate a secret you think may have leaked: the old one stops working for new tokens at once. Revoke a client you no longer use: it stops working at once, and can't be undone.
  • Use a key pair instead of a secret where your system supports it (private_key_jwt): you register only its public key, and it signs each sign-in with a private key that never leaves it. Its secret stops working; Use a secret instead goes back.
  • Review your API clients alongside your users: the page shows when each was last used.
  • API clients must sign with a key pair, at the top of the page, refuses every client still using a secret. Turning it on applies at once; turning it off waits for a second administrator.

Webhooks

A webhook tells one of your systems the moment something happens: an exception is raised, a decision is made, or a bulk upload finishes. Set one up under Admin → Webhooks with the address your system receives on and the events it wants, and give your developers the signing secret shown when you create it (shown only once).

  • Each message carries only ids; your system reads the details through the API with an API client.
  • A message your system doesn't accept is tried again for about a day. The endpoint's page shows each delivery and lets you send a failed one again, or send a test.
  • The address must be a public https:// one.

Required security keys

Under Settings → Required security keys you can require everyone who signs in with a password to also use a security key or passkey. A laptop's fingerprint reader or Windows Hello counts, as does a phone. People who sign in through your identity provider, or from online banking, aren't affected: those sign-ins are checked there.

  • Grace period. When you turn it on, people without a key get a number of days (14 unless you choose otherwise, at most 90) during which they can still sign in and see a reminder with the days left. After it, they add a key as part of signing in.
  • Customers. A customer can require keys for its own people even if you don't, from its password policy page.
  • Dual control. Turning it on, or shortening the grace period, applies at once. Turning it off, or lengthening the grace period, waits for a second administrator.
  • Lost keys. If someone loses their key, an administrator removes their keys (Users → edit → Reset security keys, or People for a customer's own administrators). First confirm it's really them, the way you would for a password reset, and say how: it goes in the audit log. They're signed out everywhere, emailed, and add a new key when they next sign in. If your only administrator loses their key, ask your service provider: two of its operators must agree before they can reset it.
  • Passwords. With a key required, a password is no longer the only factor, and NIST allows an 8-character minimum. The password policy page says so; lowering the minimum waits for a second administrator.

The Controls page warns while keys aren't required, and while people are still in the grace period without one.

Account lockout

An account locks automatically after too many failed login attempts, as a brute-force protection. A locked account can be unlocked early by an administrator from Users. The affected user always receives a lockout email regardless of their own notification preferences, since it's a security event they need to know about — the follow-up unlock notification, once an administrator resolves it, does respect the user's own notification preferences.

Sessions and signing out

Signing in starts a session. While someone is using OpenClear, the session renews automatically. If they're inactive for the idle sign-out time set on Settings, they're signed out, after a 5-minute warning with a Stay signed in button. However active they are, a session ends at the maximum session length, and they're warned to save their work before it does.

Logging out ends that session on the server, not just in the browser, so a copy of its cookies stops working too. Logging out on one device doesn't sign the person out anywhere else.

To end every session someone has, on every device:

  • An administrator can use Sign out everywhere on Users, for example after a lost laptop. This also signs the person out of any other organizations they belong to, since the sessions belong to the person. They can sign straight back in.
  • Users can do the same from Security → Sign out other devices, which keeps their current session.
  • Changing a password does it automatically, and so does an administrator password reset.

Changing and resetting passwords

Users change their own password from Security → Change password. They must enter their current password, and a wrong one counts toward the same lockout as a failed sign-in.

Each person has one password, even if they belong to more than one organization, so a new password must meet the strictest password rules of every organization and customer they belong to.

There's no self-service "forgot password" link. If someone forgets their password, an administrator with the Manage users permission can use Reset password on Users:

  • OpenClear emails the person a link to choose a new password. It works once and expires after 24 hours. You never see or choose their password, so nobody else ever knows it.
  • The user's old password stops working at once and they're signed out everywhere.
  • Resetting also clears any lockout. Resetting again replaces an unused link.
  • The reset needs email to be set up for your installation; without it, OpenClear says so and changes nothing.

When someone signs in with a password that is now too short for your rules, or that has appeared in a data breach somewhere, they go straight to the Change password page until they choose a new one. New passwords are checked the same way, and can't contain OpenClear, your organization's or customer's name, or the person's email address.

An administrator can't reset a password for someone who also belongs to another organization, because the password is shared across all of them. That person must change it themselves. Administrators change their own password from Security, not from Users.

Every password change or reset is recorded in the audit log, and the user always gets an email about it, whatever their notification preferences. An unexpected one is often the first sign that an account has been taken over.
